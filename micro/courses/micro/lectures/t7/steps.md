## Revenue, costs and profit | 5 min

:::idea The idea
A firm wants the biggest **economic profit**. To find it you need three things: how much money comes in (revenue), what it costs (explicit and implicit costs), and the difference (profit).
:::

### Revenue: total, average, marginal

| What | Formula | In words |
|---|---|---|
| **Total revenue** | TR = P · Q | all the money from sales |
| **Average revenue** | AR = TR / Q = P | money per piece, always the price |
| **Marginal revenue** | MR = ΔTR / ΔQ | extra money from one more piece |

### Costs: explicit and implicit

- **Explicit costs** (accounting costs): money really spent, like materials, rent and wages.
- **Implicit costs** (opportunity costs): what the owner gives up, like the salary they could earn elsewhere or the rent they could get for their own equipment.

### Three kinds of profit

- **Accounting profit** AP = TR − explicit costs
- **Economic profit** Eπ = AP − implicit costs
- **Normal profit** = economic profit of 0 (accounting profit exactly covers the implicit costs)

:::ana Think of it like this
You run a café and the accountant says you made 300,000 CZK. But you could earn 250,000 CZK as an employee somewhere else, and rent your coffee machine out for 100,000. Your **economic** profit is 300,000 − 350,000 = −50,000. On paper you made money, but you would be better off in the other job.
:::

:::trap Exam trap
The sample test asks exactly this: TR, explicit costs, a salary elsewhere and rent for own equipment. Subtract the explicit costs first (accounting profit), then the implicit costs (economic profit). Don't forget the rent for the owner's own equipment: it counts as an implicit cost too.
:::

## The golden rule: MR = MC | 5 min

:::idea The idea
How much should the firm produce? Look at the **next** piece only. If it brings in more than it costs, make it. If it costs more than it brings in, don't.
:::

- **MC > MR** → the last piece costs more than it brings → **decrease** production (↓Q)
- **MC < MR** → the last piece brings more than it costs → **increase** production (↑Q)
- **MC = MR** → the optimal output **Q<sub>E</sub>**: the **golden rule of profit maximisation**

The golden rule holds in **perfect competition and in imperfect competition**. Only the shape of MR changes.

:::ana Think of it like this
You sell lemonade. One more cup costs you 8 CZK in lemons and sugar (MC) and you sell it for 10 CZK (MR). Make it: +2 CZK. When your lemons run low, the next cup costs you 12 CZK (you have to buy at the expensive shop). Now MC > MR: stop.
:::

:::teach What the lecturer stresses
The goal of the firm is maximisation of profit, which goes together with minimisation of costs. The rule MR = MC answers "How much to produce?" in every market structure.
:::

## Perfect competition: the price-taker | 5 min

:::idea The idea
In perfect competition there are so many small firms selling the same thing that **no single firm can change the price**. The market (supply and demand of the whole industry) sets the price. Each firm just **takes** it.
:::

### Characteristics of perfect competition (from the lecture)

- mostly a model (an abstraction), closest to markets for unprocessed agricultural products
- the product is **homogeneous** (identical)
- **many small firms and many buyers**
- no one can determine the price: sellers are **price-takers**
- sellers act independently of each other
- firms can **enter and leave** the industry freely
- sellers and buyers are **well informed**
- transaction costs are low

### What this means for revenue

The firm can sell as much as it wants at the market price, so its demand curve is a **horizontal line** at P (perfectly elastic). Every extra piece brings exactly P:

**d = AR = MR = P**

Total revenue TR = P · Q is a **straight line from the origin** with slope P.

:::

## The optimum: P = MC, profit or loss | 6 min

:::idea The idea
Golden rule MR = MC, and for a price-taker MR = P. So the perfectly competitive firm produces where **P = MC**: draw the price line and go down from where it cuts the rising MC curve.
:::

Then compare the price with **AC** at that output to see the result:

| Result | When | Area in the graph |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic profit** (Eπ > 0) | P > AC | rectangle (P − AC) · Q above AC |
| **Normal profit** (Eπ = 0) | P = AC | no rectangle |
| **Loss** (Eπ < 0) | P < AC | rectangle (AC − P) · Q below AC |

Profit in numbers: **π = TR − TC = (P − AC) · Q**.

Move the price in the graph. The green rectangle is profit, the red one is loss.

## Short run: keep going or shut down? | 6 min

:::idea The idea
In the short run the firm must pay its **fixed costs** whether it produces or not. So the real question is: does producing at least cover the **variable** costs?
:::

- **P > AVC** → revenue covers all variable costs and part of FC → **keep producing**. The loss is **smaller than FC**.
- **P < AVC** → each piece loses money even before FC → **shut down** (Q = 0). The loss is **FC**.

### The shut-down point

**TR = VC → the loss equals FC.** Divide by Q: TR/Q = VC/Q → **AR = P = AVC**. The firm doesn't produce if **P ≤ AVC**.

The lecture also says: the shut-down point is where the marginal revenue line (the price line) **touches the AVC curve** at its minimum.

:::ana Think of it like this
You rent a market stall for the season: 10,000 CZK, already paid (fixed cost). On a rainy day you sell 900 CZK of vegetables that cost you 600 CZK. You still lose money on the season, but staying open today covers the vegetables and pays back 300 CZK of the stall. If you would sell only 500 CZK, stay home.
:::

### The supply curve comes from MC

For every price the firm produces where P = MC, but only while P ≥ min AVC. So:

- **Firm's short-run supply curve** = its **MC curve above min AVC**
- **Industry supply curve** = add every firm's quantity at each price: **S = ΣMC**

## Long run: the break-even point | 5 min

:::idea The idea
In the long run firms can **enter and leave** the industry freely. Profit attracts new firms, losses drive firms out. So economic profit keeps getting pushed towards **zero**.
:::

- **Eπ > 0** → not stable: new firms enter, market supply rises, the price falls
- **Eπ < 0** → not stable: firms leave, market supply falls, the price rises
- **Eπ = 0** → stable: **TR = TC**, or **AR = AC**

### The break-even point

The point where **revenue equals cost** → **normal profit** (profit at the level of implicit costs). For the firm: **P = min AC**.

In the lecture's words, it is the critical point where the result of the firm changes **from a profit to a loss or the other way round**.

| | Shut-down point | Break-even point |
|---|---|---|
| Condition | P = min AVC | P = min AC |
| Revenue | TR = VC | TR = TC |
| Result | loss = FC | economic profit = 0 |
| Time | short run | long run |

:::trap Exam trap
Students mix these up. **AVC → shut-down** (the V of variable, short run). **AC → break-even** (all costs, long run).
:::

:::teach Seminar 6, homework 2
A profit-maximising business loses 10,000 a year, and its fixed cost is 15,000. Short run: the loss is smaller than FC, so it covers its variable costs → **keep producing**. Long run: there are no fixed costs and it still makes a loss → **exit** the industry.
:::
