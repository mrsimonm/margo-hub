## Market equilibrium and efficiency | 5 min

:::idea The idea
The market finds its own price. Buyers want a low price, sellers want a high one. The **equilibrium price** is the compromise where the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer: **Qd = Qs**.
:::

From the lecture:

- **Market equilibrium** is the situation where consumers are willing to buy, and suppliers are willing to sell, a certain quantity at a price acceptable to both.
- In the graph it is the **intersection of the supply and demand curves**, the **equilibrium point E**.
- Sellers who want a price **above** equilibrium end up with a **surplus** of goods, so the price is pushed **down**.
- Buyers who want to pay **below** equilibrium cause a **shortage**, so the price is pushed **up**.
- Shifts of one or both curves move the equilibrium point.

### Perfect competition is efficient

In perfect competition the price equals marginal utility for buyers and marginal cost for firms. So the last unit sold is worth exactly what it costs to make:

**Allocative efficiency → MU = MC**

:::ana Think of it like this
A farmers' market at closing time. If the strawberries are too expensive, boxes are left over (surplus) and the farmer cuts the price. If they are too cheap, they're gone in ten minutes and people are still asking (shortage), so next week the price goes up.
:::

## Who gains from trade: CS and PS | 6 min

:::idea The idea
Both sides gain from taking part in a market. Buyers pay less than the good is worth to them (**consumer surplus**). Sellers get more than it costs them to make it (**producer surplus**).
:::

- **Consumer surplus** CS = TU − real expenditure (TU = ΣMU). In the graph: the triangle **under demand, above the price**.
- **Producer surplus** PS = Σ(P − MC) = TR − VC. In the graph: the triangle **above supply, under the price**.
- At the equilibrium of a perfectly competitive market **CS + PS is as big as it can be**.

## What moves the equilibrium | 6 min

:::idea The idea
When a curve shifts, the market moves to a new equilibrium. **Three things** decide how much the price and the quantity change: the time period, which shift is bigger, and the elasticity of demand.
:::

### 1. The time period

- **Very short run**: supply is fixed (a **vertical** supply curve). More demand only raises the price.
- **Short run**: firms can produce a bit more; supply slopes up.
- **Long run**: firms can expand and new firms enter; supply is flatter (more elastic). The price rises less, the quantity more.

### 2. Both curves shift at once

- **ΔS > ΔD → P falls**
- **ΔD > ΔS → P rises**

If both grow, the quantity rises for sure. The price depends on which shift is bigger.

### 3. Elastic or inelastic demand

When supply changes:

- **elastic** demand (flat): the quantity changes a lot, more than the price
- **inelastic** demand (steep): the quantity hardly changes, the price takes the hit

Try it: set ΔD to 0, move ΔS, then switch between elastic and inelastic demand.

## State interventions: ceiling, floor, tax | 5 min

:::idea The idea
Sometimes the state doesn't let the market find its price. A fixed price only **matters** if it is on the "wrong" side of equilibrium, and then one side of the market is left unsatisfied.
:::

| | Price ceiling | Price floor |
|---|---|---|
| What | maximum price | minimum price |
| Where | **below** equilibrium | **above** equilibrium |
| Result | Qs < Qd: **shortage** | Qd < Qs: **surplus**, overproduction |
| Example | regulated rents | subsidised prices of agricultural produce |

Both create a **deadweight loss**: fewer units are traded than at equilibrium.

## Imperfect competition: what changes | 5 min

:::idea The idea
In imperfect competition there is **at least one seller who can affect the price**. If it wants to sell more, it has to **lower the price**, so it faces a **downward-sloping demand curve d**.
:::

### Characteristics

- at least one seller can affect the price
- **product differentiation**
- the firm must lower the price to sell more → downward-sloping demand d

### Forms of imperfect competition

- **monopoly**
- **oligopoly**
- **monopolistic competition**

### Why does it arise?

cost conditions · barriers to competition · product differentiation · legal restrictions · asymmetric information · state intervention · the political situation · exclusive ownership of a key resource

### Same as in perfect competition

- profit **π = TR − TC**
- optimal output where **MR = MC** (the golden rule)
- the **cost curves** are the same (SR: TC = FC + VC; LR: TC = VC)
- **AR = P = d**

## Imperfect competition: MR, price, inefficiency | 6 min

:::idea The idea
Because the firm must cut the price to sell one more unit, **MR is lower than the price**. The firm still produces where **MR = MC**, then reads the price off its **demand curve**. That price is **above MC**.
:::

### The differences

- **MR < P**. For a straight demand P = a − bQ: **MR = a − 2bQ** (same start, twice as steep).
- **Monopoly power**, the **Lerner index**: **L = (P − MC) / P**, between 0 and 1.
- **P > MC** → a **deadweight loss**: allocative inefficiency.
- **Total revenue and elasticity**: elastic part |E| > 1 → MR > 0; |E| = 1 → TR is at its maximum; inelastic part → MR < 0.
- **No individual supply curve**: the relationship between P and Q is ambiguous.
- **Normal profit**: in perfect competition at Q = min AC; in imperfect competition **before** min AC.

Find Q where MR = MC, go **up** to demand for the price, and compare with the perfect-competition point (P = MC).
